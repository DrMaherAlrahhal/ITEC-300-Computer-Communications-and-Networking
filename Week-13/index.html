01 · The big problem

What makes a network a WAN?

Your university has a LAN inside each building. But its offices are in different cities. A Wide Area Network (WAN) connects LANs across large geographical areas, typically using services from telecommunications providers or the Internet.

02 · Connection strategy

Dedicated-circuit network: a reserved connection

With a dedicated circuit (such as a leased line), a provider supplies a fixed connection between locations. The contracted capacity is available for that customer rather than being dynamically allocated among customers on a shared Internet access service.

✅ Often chosen for

Predictable service capacity, provider service-level agreements, and business-critical site connectivity.

⚠️ Design trade-offs

Higher recurring cost, provisioning time, and the need for a backup path. A dedicated circuit does not automatically encrypt data.

03 · The Internet approach

Packet switching: data travels in pieces

A packet-switched network breaks data into packets. Network devices forward packets based on addressing and routing information. Different packets can experience different delays or losses; the receiving system may need to reorder or recover data.

✅ Strengths

Efficient sharing of network resources, flexibility, and multiple routes when the network supports them.

⚠️ Trade-offs

Variable delay, packet loss, and dependence on congestion management. Reliable delivery may require recovery by higher-layer protocols such as TCP.

04 · Security across public networks

VPN: a protected tunnel over another network

A Virtual Private Network (VPN) can provide authenticated, encrypted communication across an untrusted network such as the public Internet. A site-to-site VPN connects office networks; a remote-access VPN connects an individual device to organizational resources.

✅ Best-practice WAN design checklist
  • Document site locations, applications, traffic patterns, and required performance.
  • Choose circuits and providers based on availability, latency, bandwidth, cost, and SLAs.
  • Use encryption and strong authentication for sensitive traffic over untrusted networks.
  • Provide independent backup connectivity for critical sites and test failover.
  • Use sensible routing, traffic prioritization where needed, and monitoring.
  • Plan for scalability, provider outages, security controls, and ongoing costs.

The suggestions are educational starting points, not production-ready network engineering specifications.
